# Illuminated: The Image of God, Embodied

Oct 5, 2026 · @Wietske Hoencamp

*Come to him and be enlightened, and your faces shall not be confounded.* (Psalm 33:6, Douay-Rheims)

## How to read this

This booklet makes one claim and then builds a life on it. The claim: you are made in the image of God, that image is embodied, and every part of your ordinary life is the material in which it is meant to shine. Your sleep, your finances, your calendar, your speech and your kitchen table are not beside the spiritual life. They are where it happens.

It gathers two earlier works into one line of thought, and then draws a single model from them. *Imago Dei Incorporata* supplies the theology: the image given, made flesh, taken into a Body, and put to work. *Accedite et Illuminamini* supplies the path: illumination, the twelve fields of stewardship, the rule of life. Chapter 6 puts the whole of it on one page: one Master, one steward, twelve fields, four movements.

Three authorities stand behind every page, in this order. Sacred Scripture, read within the Church's Tradition, is the ground. The Magisterium is the spine: here chiefly the Catechism of the Catholic Church, whose paragraph numbers appear in brackets so that every claim can be checked, and the teaching of the popes up to Leo XIV. The Doctors of the Church are the chorus.

A fourth voice is here without being named. For a century and more, people have studied how habits form, how attention is kept, how goals are reached and how grief is carried. Much of what they found is true, and whatever is true belongs to God. This booklet takes that practical wisdom and sets it where it belongs: under grace, inside the virtues, and aimed at love. Technique serves here. It does not rule.

The booklet has a companion, the Illuminated Life app. The last two chapters show exactly how each idea becomes a screen, and how to build your own rule inside it. Read the theology first. An app without it is only a task list with a candle on it.

**A necessary caution.** Spiritual growth and psychological healing are related and they are not the same. If you carry trauma, depression, addiction, disordered eating or thoughts of harming yourself, this is not your treatment plan. If you are thinking of harming yourself, talk to someone today: your doctor, or a crisis line in your country. Work with a qualified professional and let this accompany that work. God heals bodies through physicians and minds through the healing arts, and refusing those means is not trust.

## 1. One Light

*God is light, and in him there is no darkness.* (1 John 1:5)

Before you did anything, you were something. Scripture says so on its first page, before any law is given and before any failure: God made the human being in his own image and likeness (Genesis 1:26-27). Everything else in creation is made according to its kind. Only this creature is made according to God.

The Catechism draws the consequence without hedging. Of all visible creatures, only the human person can know and love the Creator, and is the only creature on earth that God willed for its own sake \[CCC 356\]. Not as an instrument. Not for what it produces. For itself.

### What the image is

The image is not a reward, and no sin can remove it. Sin can disfigure it, and grace restores it \[CCC 705, 1701\]. A newborn bears it, and so does a person in a coma, a prisoner, and someone who has never once thought about God. Leo XIV repeats the Church's settled teaching in *Magnifica Humanitas*: every person holds a dignity grounded in their very being, which no circumstance can remove (53). What varies between people is not whether the image is there. It is how far it has been restored and brought to light.

The tradition keeps an old and useful distinction. The image is what you are and cannot lose. The likeness is what grows or withers according to how you live. Something in you is fixed, and something in you is being formed. This whole booklet lives in the space between those two sentences.

The image is also embodied. Genesis does not say a soul was made in God's image and then lodged in a body. The whole creature, shaped from the ground and breathed into, is the image. Soul and body form one nature \[CCC 365\], and the body shares in the dignity of the image \[CCC 364\]. You do not have a body the way you have a coat. You are body and soul together, one person. The soul is where the image shines most, and the body shares in it \[CCC 363-364\].

And the image is relational. The God whose likeness you bear is not solitary. He is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, an eternal exchange of self-gift. A creature made in that likeness is made for communion, and finds itself only by giving itself away. Leo XIV grounds his whole social teaching here: the person images a God who is communion, and so is created for communion with God, with others and with creation (*Magnifica Humanitas* 48-50).

### What the image is not

Four errors are alive, and each does damage.

**It is not the mind alone.** If the real person is only a stream of thought, then the sleeping, the unborn and the profoundly disabled are less than persons. The Church says the opposite.

**It is not usefulness.** Any system that prices a human being by output is at war with the first page of the Bible. That includes the private system by which you price yourself.

**It is not moral performance.** Guilt is real, and guilt does not delete the image. There is always something left for grace to catch hold of.

**It is not a possession.** A mirror does not own the light in it. Turned toward the sun it fills. Turned away it goes dark, though its surface is unchanged. The health of the image is a matter of direction, not inventory.

### Illumination

That last error gives this booklet its central word. To be illuminated is not to have an experience. It is not a rush of clarity at three in the morning. It is what happens to a whole person, mind, will and body, as they are slowly turned to face the Light and begin to carry it.

The word comes from the scriptorium. An illuminated page is not a page with pictures added. It is a page where the letter itself has been opened, gilded and made to carry light. An illuminated life is the same work done on a person. Nothing of yours is thrown away. And something is given that was never yours by nature: God's own life, poured in by grace \[CCC 1997-1999\]. The page is opened, and the gold comes from outside it.

The first Christians gave this very name to Baptism. They called it enlightenment, because the one baptised receives the Word, the true light, and becomes a child of light \[CCC 1216\]. So illumination is not first something you achieve. It was begun in you at the font.

Gold leaf will not hold on a rough surface. It needs a prepared ground. Most of what follows, the sleep and the examen and the kept promise, is the preparing of the ground. The gilding is not your work, and it never was. Even the preparing is begun and carried by grace \[CCC 2001\].

There is a restlessness in every human heart that no finished project, no relationship and no achievement closes for long. The Catechism opens on exactly this note: the desire for God is written in the human heart, and only in God will a person find the truth and happiness they never stop seeking \[CCC 27\]. The Church does not begin with an obligation. She begins with an ache and an explanation of it. The restlessness is not a defect. It is a direction.

**A word to keep.** I am a mirror, not a lamp. My part is to let him turn me.

## 2. The Light Made Flesh

*The Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us.* (John 1:14)

An account of the person that stops at dignity is a compliment, and being alive does not feel like a compliment. We see the good and choose the other thing. We love good things in the wrong order. Something is wrong, and we know it.

### The image dimmed

The Church's name for this is original sin, and it is a more hopeful teaching than it sounds. Pessimism would say this is simply what human beings are. The Church says it is not what we are. We are living below our own nature, and the gap is a wound, not a design. Our first parents lost the holiness and justice in which they were made, and we are born without it \[CCC 404\]. Baptism gives back the life of grace, and the wound remains to be healed \[CCC 405, 1263\].

The Catechism describes the first sin as a death of trust: the human being let confidence in the Creator die in the heart \[CCC 397\]. Disobedience came second. First came the suspicion that God is a rival and not a giver. Every later sin has that shape. It is rarely a love of evil. It is a good thing seized on the wrong terms, because we stopped believing it would be given.

The damage is real and it is not total. Human nature is wounded, inclined to sin, and not wholly corrupted \[CCC 405\]. A dislocated limb is still a limb. Even after baptism an inclination to sin remains, left for us to wrestle with \[CCC 1264\]. This matters pastorally in both directions. The inclination is not a licence. Its presence is not a failure either. The person who is still fighting is not thereby losing.

The wound shows up in four places, and the rest of this booklet answers each. We forget our dignity, and accept habits beneath it. We are divided, wanting and knowing pulling apart. We are isolated, treating others as rivals or resources. And we feel futile, as if our work goes nowhere.

### What God did about it

The answer the Church gives is not a philosophy. It is an event, and the event is a body. The Son of God took a human nature to accomplish our salvation in it \[CCC 461\]. He worked with human hands, thought with a human mind, chose with a human will and loved with a human heart \[CCC 470\].

The purpose was larger than pardon. The Son of God became man so that we might become children of God, sharers in the divine nature \[CCC 460\]. The Incarnation does not only forgive you. It includes you.

And he finished the work on a cross. By his death he atoned for our sins, and by his rising he opened to us a new life \[CCC 613-618, 654\]. The body he took is the body he gave.

Saint Gregory of Nazianzus drew from this one of the most consoling principles in the whole tradition: what is not assumed is not healed. Christ took all of human nature so that all of it could be restored. Turn that around and it becomes a method. If it belongs to being human, he took it up. If it is sin, he carried it to the cross.

He took a body that got tired, and slept in a boat through a storm. He took hunger and thirst. He took a human mind that learned, asked questions and grew in wisdom (Luke 2:52), while in that same mind he knew his Father and all he was sent to reveal \[CCC 472-474\]. He took grief, and wept at the grave of a friend he was about to raise. He took anger at what harms people. He took dread, and asked that the cup pass. He prayed our cry of abandonment from the cross, in our name, and was never separated from the Father \[CCC 603\]. He took death, and a real burial. The one thing he did not take is sin, because sin is not part of human nature. It is the wound in it.

So the instinct to fix yourself first and come to God afterwards is universal, and it is wrong. You do not have to compose yourself before you approach. The composure is what the approach is for.

### What follows from it

Four consequences follow, and the rest of the booklet stands on them.

**Matter can carry God.** If God took flesh, then flesh, bread, water, oil, wood and paint are not obstacles to him. This is the ground of the sacraments, and of every beautiful thing made in his honour.

**The particular is not beneath God.** He did not become humanity in general. He became one man in one town, with a mother, a trade and a Galilean accent. Your particular Tuesday is the kind of thing he inhabits.

**The body is not a problem.** God made it, took it, and means to raise it. The Creed says resurrection of the body, and means it. Contempt for the body is not humility. It is a quiet argument that God chose badly. So is the cult of the body, which asks flesh to be a god.

**Nothing human is written off.** He spent some thirty years in a workshop and a household, about which the Gospels say almost nothing. Obscurity, routine, manual work and family life have all been lived by God. All of it can now carry him.

Leo XIV opens his encyclical on this same hinge: only in the mystery of the Word made flesh does the mystery of the human person become clear (*Magnifica Humanitas* 1). In an age that offers to improve us by leaving the body behind, the Church keeps saying that our limits are not an insult. The same encyclical teaches that human beings often flourish through their limits and not in spite of them (118). We are finite, and God took the finite to himself without ceasing to be God.

**A word to keep.** Nothing in me is too damaged to bring to him. He took my nature, and he bore my sins.

## 3. Taken into a Body

*As the body is one and has many members, so also is Christ.* (1 Corinthians 12:12)

The word in this booklet's title that does the most work is the last one. *Incorporata*: taken into a body. The New Testament's word for what happens to a person joined to Christ is not only "converted". It is "incorporated", built in, grafted on, made a member.

### Not saved alone

Saint Paul does not say the Church is like a body. He says she is the Body of Christ, and he pushes the image where a mere comparison would break. No limb can say it has no need of the others. The weaker parts receive the greater care. When one suffers, all suffer, by anatomy and not by sympathy. The Church is not merely gathered around Christ. She is united in him, in his Body \[CCC 789\].

This is not a policy about attendance. It is a claim about how healing works. The interior life is not self-correcting. A person who prays alone, reads alone and discerns alone has no way to detect the drift of their own judgement, because the drift is invisible from inside. A self curved in on itself cannot uncurve itself by an operation it performs on itself. Leo XIV repeats, with Pope Francis, five words that deserve to be learned by heart: no one is saved alone (*Magnifica Humanitas* 73).

The Church is careful about what this does not mean. Those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ or his Church, yet seek God sincerely and try to do his will, may reach salvation \[CCC 847\]. All salvation comes from Christ the Head through the Church, which is his Body \[CCC 846\]. In ways known to himself, God can lead those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel to the faith without which it is impossible to please him, and so the Church keeps both the duty and the right to bring the Gospel to everyone \[CCC 848\].

You also do not choose the other limbs. A club is made of people who picked each other. A body is not. The parish contains people whose taste, politics and way of praying will grate on you for life. That friction is not a flaw in the design. There are virtues, patience first among them, that grow only among other people.

### Where matter carries God

If God took flesh, the sacraments are not an eccentric addition to Christianity. They are its consistent application. Christ now acts through signs the senses can perceive, which bring about the grace they signify \[CCC 1084\]. Water, oil, bread, wine, spoken words, a hand laid on a head.

None of the seven invents a new area of life. Each one meets something already happening: being born, growing up, failing, falling ill, marrying, serving, needing food. They touch every stage and every important moment of a Christian life \[CCC 1210\]. God deals with us according to the nature he gave us, not the one we might prefer to have.

At the centre is the Eucharist, source and summit of the whole Christian life \[CCC 1324\]. And beside it stands a warning the Fathers never tire of. Saint John Chrysostom spoke of two altars: the altar of stone in the sanctuary, and the altar of the poor, which you meet at the church door and in every street. The Lord who is honoured on the first asks to be honoured on the second. Leo XIV's first exhortation, *Dilexi Te*, is one long statement of it: love for Christ cannot be separated from love for the poor, and contact with the lowly is a fundamental way of meeting the Lord (5). A devotion that does not turn into mercy has mistaken its object.

Notice an order the ancient Church often followed with the newly baptised. She let them undergo the mysteries and explained them afterwards. Some things can only be understood from inside. Prayer is one. Marriage is another. So the counsel is not "believe without thinking". It is "do the practice, then ask what it meant", and expect the answer to deepen for decades.

### The chorus

A body reaches across space. The Church also reaches across time. The communion of saints means that those who have died in Christ are still members, still praying, still available \[CCC 946\]. The Church's memory is a resource, not an archive.

Thirty-eight of her teachers have been named Doctors, the latest being Saint John Henry Newman in 2025. They do not agree on everything. One is fierce and one is gentle. One builds cathedrals of argument and one only sings. That range is a gift. Where they all converge, you are standing on rock: God is love, grace comes first, prayer is not optional, the poor have a claim, pride is the root and humility the door. Where they differ on matters the Church has left open, you have been given a choice and not a problem.

They are also a cure for nostalgia. Read across them and you cannot believe in a golden age. Every century they describe was in trouble. Every century also had saints. Both facts are true, and holding both is the only way to be loyal and honest together.

### What this asks of you

Three plain things, and the app will ask you for each.

A parish, by name. Not Catholicism in general.

The sacraments at fixed intervals, whatever you feel. Sunday Mass. Confession on a set rhythm, because the need becomes undeniable long after it has become real.

One person with the standing to tell you no, and the practice of letting them.

**A word to keep.** I cannot uncurve myself. I can let myself be joined.

## 4. One Steward

*It is required of stewards that they be found faithful.* (1 Corinthians 4:2)

The parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30) is not about money, and it is not about productivity. It is about a servant entrusted with something that belongs to another, who will be asked about it. That is the structure of a human life.

Four sentences hold the whole doctrine of stewardship.

**Nothing you have is originally yours.** Your body, your years, your mind, your people, your gifts and your money were all received. What do you have that you did not receive? (1 Corinthians 4:7)

**All of it is on loan.** Ownership in the Christian sense is real, and it is the ownership of a trustee. God destined the earth and its goods for the whole human race, and the right to private property does not cancel that first gift \[CCC 2402-2404\].

**All of it is material for love.** The talents are not given to be kept safe. The servant who is condemned is the one who returned his talent intact and unused. An image that does nothing is an image in storage.

**The accounting is real.** Saint John of the Cross says that at the evening of life we shall be judged on our love \[CCC 1022\]. Not on output, influence or how well our plans turned out. On love. The Church means this soberly. Each of us meets Christ in a particular judgement at death, and the parable does not hide that the gift can be refused and lost \[CCC 1021, 1033-1037\]. The Judge is the same Lord who died for the servant he questions.

### Steward, not owner, and not slave

There are two ways to get this wrong, and most people swing between them.

The owner says: this is mine, and I answer to no one. Owners grip. Their plans cannot be interrupted, their money cannot be asked for, and a reversal undoes them, because it touches the one thing never handed over, which is control.

The slave says: I must earn my place. Slaves strive. Their worth rises and falls with the week's results, rest feels like theft, and failure feels like being erased.

The steward stands in a third place, and it is a place of freedom. The steward's worth was settled before the work began. That is Chapter 1. So the work can be done wholeheartedly and held lightly. A steward can be corrected without collapse, can stop without guilt, and can hand the work on, because it was never theirs to keep.

This is also why the master in the parable says the same words to the servant with five talents and the servant with two. The measure is not the amount. It is faithfulness with what was actually given. You are not asked about anyone else's field.

### One steward

The second word in this chapter's title matters as much as the first. You are one.

Much exhaustion in serious people does not come from doing too much. It comes from being several people at once, a work self, a church self, a private self, and paying the upkeep on each. The Lord's own image for the cure is the single eye: if your eye is sound, your whole body will be full of light (Matthew 6:22). An undivided aim makes the whole person luminous.

So the fields in the next chapter are not twelve projects. They are twelve places where one love is at work. Saint Augustine called virtue the right ordering of love. Sin, on his account, is seldom love of a bad thing. It is a good thing loved out of its place: work loved as identity, a friend loved as saviour, comfort loved above duty. The question to ask of any attachment is not "do I love this too much?", which only breeds scruples. It is "what does this outrank in my life?" That question has an answer, because behaviour settles it. Look at where the money goes when it is tight, and what gets cancelled when the week is full.

The order itself is simple to state. God first, as the love that puts every other love in its place and so makes it safe. Then those directly entrusted to you: spouse, children, parents, the people who depend on you. The Catechism roots this in the fourth commandment and calls it the order of charity \[CCC 2197\]. Then the wider circles of friendship, work, neighbour and stranger, with the poor holding a claim that is not optional \[CCC 2443-2449\]. Your state of life gives this order its concrete shape. Married, single, ordained or consecrated, all are called to the same holiness, and each walks toward it through the duties of that state \[CCC 2013\].

### How God asks

One last thing before the map. God is a patient teacher with a method. The Catechism calls it the divine pedagogy: he reveals himself by stages, at the pace his people can bear \[CCC 53\]. He begins with the senses, with bread and water and fire. He teaches through relationship and not by data. He never coerces. He aims at freedom and not at compliance.

It follows that he ordinarily asks about one field at a time. When your method with yourself is harsher or faster than his, yours is wrong.

**A word to keep.** Nothing I have is mine. All of it is material for love, and I will be asked.

## 5. The Twelve Fields

*Well done, good and faithful servant. You have been faithful over a little.* (Matthew 25:21)

Here is the map of everything you have been given. Its purpose is diagnostic. Most people are strong in three or four fields, adequate in five, and have one they have quietly written off. The written-off field is usually where the next growth is.

For each field: what it holds, what faithfulness looks like, how it fails, the question to ask, and one small first step.

### I. Body

Sleep, food, movement, medical care, rest, sexuality. Your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit. You are not your own, for you were bought at a price, so glorify God in your body (1 Corinthians 6:19-20). The Spirit truly dwells in the baptised, and this body is destined to rise \[CCC 364, 1004\]. That is why its care is worship and not vanity. Life and health are gifts entrusted to us, to be cared for reasonably, without making an idol of physical perfection \[CCC 2288-2289\]. Sexuality belongs here too. Chastity is its integration within the person, lived according to one's state of life \[CCC 2337\].

- **Faithful:** a fixed bedtime, meals at a table, a daily walk, appointments kept.

- **Fails as:** neglect on one side, the cult of the body on the other. Both treat the body as something other than you.

- **Ask:** am I caring for the body I was given, or punishing it, or ignoring it?

- **First step:** the phone charges outside the bedroom.

### II. Mind

Attention, study, formation, imagination, everything you let in. What you look at becomes what you think with.

- **Faithful:** one serious book always in progress, chosen inputs, faith that keeps learning.

- **Fails as:** a scattered attention sold by the minute, or a theology that stopped growing at eighteen.

- **Ask:** who decides what enters my mind each day?

- **First step:** twenty minutes of real reading before any screen.

### III. Soul

The faith itself, received and handed on: prayer, Scripture, sacraments, confession, silence, spiritual direction. The tradition proposes fixed rhythms of prayer precisely because constant prayer is fed by structure \[CCC 2698\].

- **Faithful:** daily anchors at set times, Sunday Mass, confession on a set interval, one director.

- **Fails as:** bursts of intensity, a retreat and then a collapse, with nothing in between.

- **Ask:** would my prayer survive my worst week?

- **First step:** two minutes, morning and night, same place.

### IV. Heart

Marriage and family, friendship, community, emotional honesty. The family is a communion of persons, an image of the communion of Father, Son and Spirit \[CCC 2205\].

- **Faithful:** presence that is not distracted, and three relationships: someone ahead of you, someone beside you, someone you are helping along.

- **Fails as:** being widely liked and known by no one.

- **Ask:** who knows the whole truth about me?

- **First step:** one unhurried hour a week, in person, in the calendar.

### V. Time

The calendar, commitments, rhythm, saying no. Your calendar is a more honest statement of your loves than your journal.

- **Faithful:** hours that match your stated priorities, rest booked first, empty space left on purpose \[CCC 2184-2185\].

- **Fails as:** a life of reaction, where the urgent permanently defeats the important.

- **Ask:** if a stranger read my week, what would they say I love?

- **First step:** block the day of rest before anything else.

### VI. Finance

Earning, spending, saving, giving, debt. The Fathers call almsgiving a work of mercy and also a work of justice: not to share with the poor is to rob them \[CCC 2446-2447\].

- **Faithful:** knowing your numbers, giving first and by proportion, contentment practised as a skill.

- **Fails as:** vagueness, which is nearly always avoidance dressed as detachment.

- **Ask:** do I know, to the euro, what came in and what I gave?

- **First step:** set the giving aside on the day income arrives.

### VII. Work

Labour, craft, profession, excellence. Work is a share in the work of creation, and its dignity comes from the person who does it \[CCC 2427-2428\].

- **Faithful:** done well, on time, honestly, and then stopped.

- **Fails as:** work as identity, where rest is impossible because you would have to meet yourself.

- **Ask:** does my work leave me able to pray and to love the people at home?

- **First step:** a shutdown ritual: write tomorrow's first task, close the laptop, say "enough".

### VIII. Home

Your physical space, order, hospitality, daily beauty. The home is the domestic church, the first school of Christian life \[CCC 1655-1657\].

- **Faithful:** a home that can receive a guest without an emergency, enough order to think in, one beautiful thing in each room.

- **Fails as:** the space that drains you every day and is never addressed.

- **Ask:** could I welcome someone tonight?

- **First step:** ten minutes of resetting one room each evening.

### IX. Creation

The earth, animals, food, consumption, what you buy and from whom. Dominion over creation is a stewardship that answers to God and to those who come after \[CCC 2415-2418\].

- **Faithful:** buying less and better, knowing where things come from, cutting waste as a quiet discipline.

- **Fails as:** treating it as someone else's issue.

- **Ask:** what did my household throw away this week?

- **First step:** one meal a week cooked wholly from what is already in the house.

### X. Speech

Words, honesty, gossip, promises, everything you publish. Detraction and calumny wound justice and charity, and the damage must be repaired \[CCC 2477, 2487\].

- **Faithful:** the hard thing said to the person's face and nothing behind their back; your yes meaning yes.

- **Fails as:** the pious feed and the unguarded conversation.

- **Ask:** would I say this if they were in the room?

- **First step:** one day a week with no comment on anyone who is absent.

### XI. Beauty and Making

Art, music, craft, writing, garden, song. Truth carries its own splendour, and artistic gifts bring a duty to use them \[CCC 2500-2501\].

- **Faithful:** making something regularly with no productive excuse, and receiving beauty on purpose.

- **Fails as:** "no time for that any more", the first sign of a life that has become purely useful.

- **Ask:** when did I last make something just because it was good?

- **First step:** thirty protected minutes a week at your craft.

### XII. Mission

Service, witness, the poor, your particular contribution. The works of mercy are how the image goes to work in the world \[CCC 2447\].

- **Faithful:** one concrete, standing service, sized to your present strength, where you meet actual people.

- **Fails as:** a mission that exists as a plan and not as an appointment.

- **Ask:** whom, by name, did I serve this month?

- **First step:** one recurring hour of service, small enough to keep.

### A caution about the twelve

Do not attempt all twelve. The point of the map is not excellence everywhere at once. It is knowing which field God is asking about now. Score each from one to five. Take the lowest. Choose one small habit for it, and add nothing new in the other eleven for ninety days. Their duties and the commandments still hold. Only the new projects wait.

And look for the field you skimmed while reading.

**A word to keep.** Master, make me faithful in the field I have been avoiding.

## 6. The Steward's Model

*As each has received a gift, use it to serve one another, as good stewards of God's varied grace.* (1 Peter 4:10)

Everything so far fits on one page. One Master entrusts one steward with twelve fields in three rings, and in every field the same four movements take place. That is the whole model. The rest of this chapter unfolds it.



the steward's model · one centre, three rings, twelve fields

Read it from the centre outward. Light is given at the centre, passes through the person, orders the household, and lands in the world.

### The centre

At the centre is not a goal, a vision statement or a best self. It is a Person. The model fails at once if anything else is put there, including the model.

The parable shows why. The servant who buried his talent explains himself: I knew you to be a hard man, and I was afraid (Matthew 25:24-25). His failure did not begin with laziness. It began with a false picture of the master. Fear buries. Trust invests. So the first act of stewardship is not effort. It is knowing the Master rightly: as the one who gives before he asks, and whose final word to a faithful servant is not "adequate" but "enter into the joy of your master".

### Three rings

The New Testament word for steward is *oikonomos*, the manager of a household. The model keeps that shape.

**The Person: what I am.** Body, Mind, Soul, Heart. These are the four with which the great commandment asks us to love God: heart, soul, mind and strength (Mark 12:30). They come first because you cannot give what you have not received, and because the self is the first thing entrusted to you.

**The Household: what I keep.** Time, Finance, Work, Home. These are the steward's proper desk: the hours, the goods, the labour and the place put into your hands. Here love takes structure, or stays a feeling.

**The World: what I give.** Creation, Speech, Beauty and Making, Mission. This is the second half of the commandment, the neighbour, widened to the earth itself. Here the light leaves your hands.

The rings are an order of flow and not a ranking of worth. Inner serves outer. A person who polishes the inner ring and never reaches the outer has built a lamp and put it under a basket. A person who pours out the outer ring while the inner one starves is a channel and not a reservoir, and runs dry.

In this book and in the app each ring has a colour, like the glass of a rose window: ruby for the Person, sapphire for the Household, emerald for the World. Gold is kept for the centre. The colours are an aid to memory. They are not the Church's liturgical colours.

A picture may help, and every Catholic already carries it. Think of a parish church. At its heart a lamp burns before the tabernacle. Christ is there, and that is the centre. Around him stands **the temple**, which is you: your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19). Around the temple is **the house**: the home, which the Church calls the domestic church \[CCC 1655-1657\]. Beyond the doors lies **the field**: the field is the world (Matthew 13:38), and every Mass ends by sending us into it. Lamp, temple, house, field. That is the whole model, seen from the pew.

### Four movements

In every field, faithful stewardship moves through the same four acts. They echo the four things the Lord did with bread: he took it, blessed it, broke it and gave it (Matthew 26:26). This is an analogy and not a new doctrine, but it rests on one. By baptism every Christian shares in the priesthood of Christ, and the Church teaches that the works, prayers, family life, daily labour, rest and even the hardships of the laity become spiritual sacrifices, offered to the Father with the Lord's Body in the Eucharist \[CCC 901\]. Saint Paul says the same: present your bodies as a living sacrifice (Romans 12:1). Stewardship is what the weekday brings to the altar on Sunday, where it is joined to Christ's one sacrifice by the hands of the priest \[CCC 1368\].

1.  **Receive.** Name what was given, and give thanks for it. Gratitude is not a mood. It is the accurate recognition that this was not mine first. Nothing can be stewarded that is still being treated as owned.

2.  **Bless.** Set it in its place under God. What is this field for? What does it outrank, and what outranks it? This is the ordering of love, done one field at a time.

3.  **Spend.** Put it to work at a cost. Bread that is never broken feeds no one, and a talent never risked earns nothing. Here belong the habit, the practice and the kept appointment.

4.  **Return.** Hand it back. Give it to someone, and give an account of it to God. The evening examen is the daily form of this. The final form is the evening of life.

Each movement has a counterfeit. Without Receive you get entitlement. Without Bless, disorder. Without Spend, the buried talent. Without Return, the barn-builder of Luke 12, who lays up treasure for himself and is not rich toward God (Luke 12:21).

### Christ at the centre

The centre of the model is not an idea of God. It is Jesus Christ, and Scripture says why in three sentences. God's own plan, his own stewardship, is to unite all things in Christ (Ephesians 1:10). In him all things hold together (Colossians 1:17). And Saint Paul gives the whole model in a line: all things are yours, and you are Christ's, and Christ is God's (1 Corinthians 3:21-23).

The Church reads the Old Testament as full of figures that point to him \[CCC 128-130\]. Several of them are figures of the steward.

**The new Adam.** The first man was set in a garden to till it and keep it (Genesis 2:15), and he did not keep it. Christ is the new Adam (Romans 5:14). He was obedient in a garden, and on Easter morning Mary Magdalene took him for the gardener (John 20:15). She was not entirely wrong.

**The true Temple.** He spoke of the temple of his body (John 2:21). In the city to come there is no temple, because the Lord and the Lamb are its temple, and the Lamb is its lamp (Revelation 21:22-23). We are built into him as living stones (1 Peter 2:5).

**Joseph.** Sold by his brothers, raised up, set over another man's house, he fed the nations with bread (Genesis 39-41). The Fathers saw Christ in him.

**The Son over the house.** Moses was faithful in God's house as a servant. Christ is faithful over it as a Son, and we are that house (Hebrews 3:5-6).

**The keeper of the key.** Eliakim the steward received the key of the house of David (Isaiah 22:20-22). The risen Christ holds it (Revelation 3:7).

One more image is offered as an application and not as a figure Scripture itself draws. The high priest carried twelve stones on his breastplate, on his heart before the Lord (Exodus 28:21, 29). Those stones are the twelve tribes, fulfilled in the Church. But the steward may take courage from them. You do not carry your twelve fields alone. The High Priest carries you, and all you hold, on his heart.

### Three threads through all twelve

Scripture names three things that are not fields. They run through every field.

**The temple: what you are.** Before you hold anything, you are held. By Baptism you are a temple of the Holy Spirit and a member of Christ \[CCC 1265\]. This is the dignity of Chapter 1, and no field can add to it.

**The calling: the shape your life is given.** Each one should lead the life the Lord has assigned (1 Corinthians 7:17). Your state of life and your particular call decide what faithfulness means in every field. They are discerned in prayer, over time, with the Church, and never by a book or an app.

**The gifts: what you bring.** As each has received a gift, use it to serve one another (1 Peter 4:10). The gifts of the Holy Spirit make you ready to follow him \[CCC 1830-1831\]. Charisms are given through you for others, for the building up of the Church \[CCC 799-801\]. A gift kept for oneself is a buried talent.

And one thing is entrusted before all the rest. Saint Paul calls us stewards of the mysteries of God (1 Corinthians 4:1). The faith itself, received and handed on, is the first treasure. In the map it lives in the field of the Soul, and it is given away in the field of Mission.

### In Christ, and not alone

The steward in this model is not a solitary achiever. Four truths keep the model Christian.

**Christ is the faithful Son.** He is more than a servant in the house. He is the Son set over it (Hebrews 3:6), the image of the invisible God (Colossians 1:15), who received everything from the Father and handed everything back. We do not imitate him from outside. We are members of his Body, and our stewardship is a share in his. Apart from me you can do nothing (John 15:5).

**The baptised share his three offices.** As priest, the steward offers each field to God \[CCC 901\]. As prophet, the steward bears witness in word and life \[CCC 904-905\]. As king, the steward first governs the self and then orders the world toward justice, because ruling in Christ means serving \[CCC 908-909\]. The twelve fields are where those three offices are actually exercised.

**The Holy Spirit does the work.** No one orders their loves by technique. The love of God is poured into our hearts by the Holy Spirit (Romans 5:5), and it is the Spirit who makes us children and not slaves \[CCC 736\]. His seven gifts make a person quick to follow where grace leads \[CCC 1830-1831\]. Every movement in this model begins by asking him.

**Our Lady is the model.** She received ("let it be done to me"), she blessed (the Magnificat), she spent herself (going in haste to Elizabeth), and she returned everything (standing by the Cross). No one has held more, or held it more openly. She is more than an example. She is given to us as Mother in the order of grace, and she prays for every steward who asks \[CCC 968-969\].

### Six laws

**Grace comes first, always.** Every good act begins in God's initiative. Even our preparation to receive grace is already a work of grace \[CCC 2001\], and our merits are themselves his gifts \[CCC 2008-2009\]. Effort is real, and it is always a response. A model of stewardship that forgets this has become self-salvation with a Catholic vocabulary.

**Gift comes before task.** Your worth was settled before the work began. The steward works from dignity and not toward it.

**The picture of the Master governs everything.** When a field has gone dark, look first at what you believe God is like in that field. A hard master in your head will bury your talent for you.

**One field at a time.** God teaches by stages, and so must you. Choose one field for a season of about ninety days. Add nothing new in the others, and keep their ordinary duties.

**Begin again, without penalty.** This model keeps no streaks and no score. A field is lit by degrees: not yet begun, kindled, shining, burning, radiant. When you fall out of a practice you do not start from zero and you do not catch up. You begin again where you are. His mercies are new every morning (Lamentations 3:22-23). One caution keeps this honest. The lamps record practices kept, and they do not measure grace. No one can read the state of their own soul from a chart \[CCC 2005\]. Mortal sin does break communion with God. It requires grave matter, full knowledge and deliberate consent \[CCC 1857\], and what restores communion is not a habit but the sacrament of Reconciliation \[CCC 1446, 1855-1856\]. If you are unsure where you stand, do not ask a chart. Ask a confessor.

**The test is love of neighbour.** Every inner gain must show up in the outer ring. If you are surer of God's favour and less patient with the people at your table, the work is not finished yet.

### The face of the steward

The Beatitudes show what a faithful steward looks like: poor in spirit, meek, merciful, pure of heart, a maker of peace. They paint the face of Christ, and they describe the calling of everyone joined to him \[CCC 1716-1717\]. The model is only a way of walking toward that face.

### What the model is not

It is not a wheel to be balanced. Lives are not balanced. They are ordered, and order means some things rightly come first.

It is not a ladder to climb alone. Grace begins the work, carries it and completes it, and it does so inside the Church.

And it is not a measure of you. It is a map of what you hold, drawn so that you can find the one field where the Master is knocking.

**A word to keep.** Receive, bless, spend, return. Everything I hold can be held this way.

## 7. The Craft of Change

*Be transformed by the renewal of your mind.* (Romans 12:2)

Grace does not replace nature. It heals and perfects it. So the ordinary mechanics of how people change are not beneath the spiritual life. They are its tools. The tradition calls a virtue a *habitus*: a stable disposition built by repeated acts, which makes the good easier, more reliable and at last delightful \[CCC 1803-1804, 1810\]. Human virtues are acquired by practice, and grace purifies and raises them. Faith, hope and charity cannot be acquired at all. God pours them in, and everything in this chapter depends on them \[CCC 1810-1813\]. You do not become patient by resolving to be patient. You become patient by doing patient things until the disposition sets, the way a bone sets.

What follows is the practical wisdom of formation, gathered into rules. Each is ordinary good sense. Each is also older than it looks, and each has a place in the model.

### Beginning

**Begin from the end.** Decide what the evening of your life should find, and let today answer to it. Teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom (Psalm 90:12).

**Act from who you are.** Lasting change follows identity. You are not trying to become someone acceptable. You are a baptised child of God learning to act like one.

**Start smaller than seems serious.** Whoever is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much (Luke 16:10). Two minutes kept beats twenty minutes intended. Growth follows consistency.

**Do not wait for the mood.** Feeling follows action far more often than it leads. In Lent the Church asks first for conversion of heart \[CCC 1430\], and she knows the road to it: keep the fast, give the alms, say the prayer, and let the heart follow.

### Building

**Fix the time and the place.** A practice with an hour and a chair survives. A practice that floats is decided by mood, and mood is not a reliable superior.

**Tie the new to the settled.** Prayer straight after the morning coffee. The examen straight after brushing your teeth. An anchored habit holds.

**Shape the room, not just the will.** Remove the occasion from the house instead of resisting it in the house. Put the book on the pillow and the phone in the hall. The desert monks knew this.

**One thing at a time.** One field for a season. One new practice at once. Ambition is the usual killer of consistency.

**Name the floor.** Decide in a good hour the three things you will keep in a bad one. This prevents the all-or-nothing collapse.

**Write it down, and review it.** A commitment with a date and a weekly look is a plan. Without them it is a wish.

### Attending

**Put the important before the urgent.** Martha was busy with many things. One thing is needful (Luke 10:41-42). Give the first and best hours to what lasts, and let the loud things wait.

**Guard your attention.** What you look at becomes what you think with. Choose your inputs as deliberately as your food.

**Do one thing with your whole mind.** Divided attention does several things badly and prays not at all.

**Live today.** Do not be anxious about tomorrow (Matthew 6:34). Do what is yours to do, and hand over what is not. Most worry is an attempt to carry a day that has not been given yet.

**Think on what is true.** Whatever is true, honourable, just, pure and lovely, think about these things (Philippians 4:8). Thoughts can be examined, answered and replaced. You are not obliged to believe every sentence your mind produces.

### Enduring

**Expect resistance, and read it rightly.** Difficulty is not a sign you chose wrongly. Suffering produces endurance, endurance character, and character hope (Romans 5:3-4).

**Rise gently.** When you fall, do not prosecute yourself. Get up, tell the truth, begin again. Harshness toward your own failures spends the strength you need to mend them, and it is sometimes pride, and often an old wound.

**Ask for help, daily.** Perseverance is a gift given to those who ask. Asking is not weakness in this craft. It is the method.

**Rest on purpose.** One day in seven produces nothing. A person who cannot stop has made the work a god.

### Relating

**Listen first.** Be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger (James 1:19). Understand before you ask to be understood.

**Tell the truth in love.** Say the hard thing kindly, to the person's face, and first between the two of you alone (Ephesians 4:15; Matthew 18:15). Kindness without truth flatters. Truth without kindness wounds.

**Be known by someone.** Two are better than one, for if they fall, one will lift up the other (Ecclesiastes 4:9-10). A struggle told to the right person loses half its power.

**Keep your boundaries.** Perfect love is not perpetual availability. The Lord himself withdrew from crowds to pray (Luke 5:16).

**Give first.** Give, and it will be given to you (Luke 6:38). Lead with generosity and keep no ledger.

### Holding goods

**Give off the top.** Decide the proportion in advance and set it aside the day income arrives.

**Live beneath your means, and know your numbers.** Contentment is learned (Philippians 4:11-12). Vagueness about money is rarely detachment.

**Pay what you owe, and owe no one anything except love** (Romans 13:8). Treat debt as a weight to put down.

**Keep a reserve.** Prudence stores in summer (Proverbs 6:6-8). A reserve is not a lack of faith. It is care for those who depend on you.

### Where the world's advice goes wrong

The craft is sound. The creed it often arrives with is not. Four corrections keep technique in its place.

**You are not the centre.** Much advice assumes the self is the project and the self is the judge. The steward answers to Another, and that is a relief.

**You are not limitless.** You are finite, and finite is good. A plan that denies tiredness, age or dependence is at odds with what you are.

**Wanting hard is not prayer.** God is a Father, not a mechanism that returns what is pictured clearly enough. Prayer ends in "your will be done".

**Success is not the measure.** The servants in the parable are praised for being faithful, not for being impressive. And at the centre of this faith stands a cross, which no technique removes.

Three questions test any method before you adopt it. Is it true? Does it serve love? Does it leave room for grace and for the cross?

**A word to keep.** I decided in a clear hour. Today I only keep the appointment.

## 8. When the Light Seems to Go Out

*The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it.* (John 1:5)

Any account of an ordered Christian life owes the reader a chapter on what happens when it stops working. Prayer that was warm goes dry. Convictions that were vivid become sentences you still agree with and no longer feel. The rule becomes a list of obligations. Nothing has been decided against. Everything has simply gone quiet.

The first instinct is to read this as failure. The tradition says: sometimes, and often not.

### Four ordinary causes

Rule these out first. Most darkness has an ordinary cause, and ordinary causes have ordinary remedies.

**Exhaustion.** A body that has not slept cannot pray, attend or resist. There is no spiritual reading of chronic tiredness that improves on the obvious one. Sleep, eat, go outside.

**Illness.** Depression is an illness with a physiology, and it responds to treatment. From outside it can look like spiritual dryness. It is not the same thing, though the two can come together. Ask a doctor and a priest, and let neither question wait for the other. The Church has no teaching that asks a suffering person to decline medical help, and she has a great deal of teaching on the duty to care for life and health \[CCC 2288\].

**Unaddressed sin.** A grudge kept warm, a relationship conducted dishonestly, a persistent refusal: these deaden prayer in a wholly unmysterious way. The remedy is confession, not more contemplation.

**Overcommitment.** A life with no hour in which nothing is required will find that prayer has become one more requirement. This is the occupational hazard of people who build things for God.

### The fifth cause

When those are ruled out, there remains the possibility the mystics describe. God sometimes withdraws the felt sweetness of prayer so that a person learns to love him and not his gifts. The Catechism names it plainly: dryness is the moment of sheer faith, clinging to Jesus in his agony and in his tomb \[CCC 2731\].

Saint John of the Cross gives three signs. You find no satisfaction in the things of God or in anything else. You are troubled that you may be failing him. And you can no longer meditate as you once did, however you try, while a quiet desire simply to be with God remains. Where those meet, the counsel is not to force the old feelings back. Stay. Simplify. Keep the appointment and let it be bare.

A gentle test may help. Does my darkness leave me still wanting God and still able to love? If so, it may be a purification. If it comes with panic, numbness, self-hatred or thoughts of harming yourself, treat it as an injury and get help today. Both answers are faithful.

### Rules for a dark season

1.  **Make no large changes.** Do not resign, end the relationship or send the message. States pass. Decisions made inside them do not.

2.  **Reduce the rule to its floor. Do not abandon it.** One anchor of prayer, Sunday Mass, one person who knows. If you are too ill to manage even that, the Church does not hold it against you. The floor is for dryness, not for the sickbed.

3.  **Let the basics be the discipline.** Light, movement, food, sleep and company. In this season a walk is an act of obedience to the God who made the body.

4.  **Tell one trustworthy person the whole of it.** Secrecy feeds the dark. Speech is the solvent.

5.  **Keep doing the next good thing.** The work does not require the feelings. A small act of love done while feeling nothing is worth no less than one done on a wave. Its worth is the love in it.

### Suffering is not wasted

This is the hardest claim in the faith, and it must never be said glibly to someone in the middle of it. But the Church does say it. Suffering joined to Christ becomes fruitful and not only endured. Saint Paul can write that he rejoices in his sufferings for the sake of the Body (Colossians 1:24), and the Catechism teaches that by his passion Christ gave suffering a new meaning, so that it can configure us to him and unite us with his redeeming work \[CCC 1505, 1521\].

Scripture does not fully explain suffering. God does more than explain. He enters it. For most people who are actually suffering, that turns out to be the more useful thing.

Leo XIV's prayer intention for October 2026 asks that the Church become an open house for those who struggle with their mental health, a community that accompanies and never excludes. Take that as permission in both directions. You may need to be accompanied. And someone near you needs you to stay.

**A word to keep.** The absence of feeling is not the absence of God.

## 9. The Bridge: From Booklet to App

*Be doers of the word, and not hearers only.* (James 1:22)

A book can convince you. It cannot remind you at seven in the morning. The Illuminated Life app exists for that one purpose: to turn what you now hold to be true into appointments you keep. Its whole design is self-knowledge in the presence of God, turned into times and places where love can be practised.

Nothing in the app is new. Every screen is a chapter of this booklet in working form.

The app also keeps a diary. Each evening it asks for seven things you are grateful for, three intentions you are praying for, and one act of service, done or planned. It can be saved or printed as a small book. Gratitude is the first movement, Receive, practised every day.

Two more things are in the app. **The Icon Screen** is a mirror in five panels: where you stand in prayer, which gifts of the Spirit are awake, which charisms draw you, which root sin shadows your gift, and the register of your calling. It never tells you what you are. Each result comes with an obligation and must be shown to someone who knows you. **The Guide** asks you plain questions and turns your answers into a first rule of life and a plan for the day, which you can put into your own calendar. It is a helper for drafting. It is not a spiritual director, and it says so.

### Six design promises

The app is built to obey the theology. Each promise below is a doctrine turned into a design rule.

**It lights and never scores.** There are no points, streaks or percentages of you. Dignity is not a metric (Chapter 1).

**It does not measure grace.** A lit petal records a practice kept. It says nothing about the state of your soul, and the app says so on the screen (Chapter 6).

**It asks about one field.** The other eleven stay visible and quiet (Chapter 4).

**It lets you begin again without penalty.** A missed day is simply not marked. There is nothing to catch up (Chapter 6).

**It sends you out of itself.** The parish, the confessional, the friend and the poor are not in the app. It points to them and then closes (Chapter 3).

**It keeps your words on your own device.** What you write is stored only in your browser. You can back it up to a file, print it as a booklet, and delete it at any time. Nothing is sent anywhere.

### What the app must never become

It is not a spiritual director, a confessor or a diagnosis. It cannot absolve, and it cannot discern a vocation. If it ever starts to feel like a judge instead of a trellis, switch on floor mode, or close it for a season. The anchors are enough.

## 10. Build Your Own Illuminated Life

*Unless the Lord builds the house, those who build it labour in vain.* (Psalm 127:1)

This is the one format. It takes about an hour the first time, and it is the same in the app and on paper. Do the steps in order. Do not skip the last one.

1.  **Pray first.** Before you plan anything, ask. "Lord, show me what you have given me, and where you are asking." A rule made without prayer is a self-improvement plan.

2.  **Walk the twelve fields.** For each, choose its present light: not yet begun, kindled, shining, burning or radiant. Be quick and honest. This is a glance, not a verdict.

3.  **Choose one field for the season.** Usually the dimmest, or the one you skimmed. Add nothing new in the other eleven for about ninety days.

4.  **Write its four movements.** One line each.

    - *Receive:* what have I been given here? Thank God for it by name.

    - *Bless:* what is this field for, and where does it stand among my loves?

    - *Spend:* what one small practice will put it to work? Give it a time and a place.

    - *Return:* to whom is this given, and when will I review it before God?

5.  **Set your floor.** Three lines you will keep in your worst week. For most people: two minutes of prayer morning and night, Sunday Mass, one honest conversation a week.

6.  **Fix three anchors in the day.** A morning offering before the phone. A midday pause, the Angelus or one deliberate breath and a word. An evening examen of five minutes.

7.  **Mark the week, the month and the year.** Sunday Mass and real rest. Confession on a set interval. One review of your finances. The Church's seasons kept, with one thing added and one removed in Advent and Lent.

8.  **Check the Church's own minimum.** The five precepts are the floor beneath every floor: Mass on Sundays and holy days, confession at least once a year, Communion at least in the Easter season, the days of fasting and abstinence, and providing for the needs of the Church \[CCC 2041-2043\]. Illness, the care of infants and other serious reasons excuse from the Sunday obligation \[CCC 2181\].

9.  **Put it in your one calendar.** Not a separate spiritual calendar. There is only one life. The app exports your anchors as calendar events.

10. **Show it to someone.** A director, a confessor or a wise friend. A rule written alone tends to be too harsh or too vague, and you cannot tell which from inside.

11. **Review each season, not each day.** At the end of ninety days, look again at the twelve, give thanks, and ask which field is next.

>
> Receive: \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

>

That is a complete rule of life. A person who kept only the floor for forty years, inside the sacraments, would have given grace a great deal of room. Everything else is elaboration.

## Prayers of the Steward

These are written for private use. They are not liturgical texts.

**Morning offering.** Father, everything I will touch today is yours before it is mine. I receive this day from your hand. Set my loves in order. Let me spend what you have given, and bring it back to you tonight. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

**To the Holy Spirit.** Come, Holy Spirit. I cannot set my own loves in order. Pour the love of God into my heart, and make me quick to follow where you lead. Amen.

**For the temple of my body.** Holy Spirit, you dwell in this body, and it was bought at a price. Teach me to live in it as your temple: to rest it, to feed it with thanks, to keep it pure, and to spend it in love. I look for the resurrection of the body. Amen.

**Receive.** Lord, I did not make this and I did not earn it. Thank you.

**Bless.** Lord, put this in its place beneath you, and let nothing be loved less than it deserves, or more.

**Spend.** Lord, I am afraid of losing what I hold. Give me the courage to put it to work.

**Return.** Lord, here is what you gave me, and what I did with it. Take both.

**For the field I have been avoiding.** Master, you entrusted this to me, and I have buried it because I was afraid. You are not a hard master. Show me the first small thing, and stay with me while I do it. Amen.

**In a dark season.** God beyond feeling, I do not sense you. I choose you anyway. Let that count as prayer. Amen.

**At night.** Into your hands, Lord, I commend my spirit. What I finished is yours. What I did not finish is yours too. Amen.

## A note on authority

This booklet is a private work of formation. It is not an official text of the Church and carries no imprimatur. It was written to agree in every point with Sacred Scripture and the Magisterium, and it is submitted without reserve to the Church's judgement. Where it errs, the Church's teaching governs.

Before it is published or taught from, it should be read by a priest or a theologian. The Catechism paragraph numbers are given so that every claim can be checked against the source, and they should be.

The four movements, the three rings and the image of the rose window are the booklet's own way of arranging what the Church teaches. They are aids to memory, not doctrines.
